Phase 1 / Week 1-8

Discover & Define

This phase documents how we identified the problem space, studied target users, and translated research insights into playful requirements.

The Why

Why this track

Track fit: Relation between Generations

Project focus: Low-pressure emotional connection between university students and parents living apart.

The Gap

Research gap

Existing tools often support messaging, safety awareness, or memory sharing separately. Kinlight focuses on the missing space between them: soft awareness, lightweight expression, and gentle conversation support for parent-student communication.

Stakeholder Scope

Stakeholders

Primary users: University students living away from home and their parents.

Secondary users: Wider family members who may later join shared family spaces.

Read full motivation statement

Kinlight was developed for the Social Connectivity / Relation between Generations track because parent-student communication often becomes more fragile after young adults move away from home. In this situation, both sides may still want emotional closeness, but everyday contact can become difficult: students may feel pressured by frequent questions or long replies, while parents may worry that asking too much will interrupt or control their child. Existing communication tools support messaging, photo sharing, or family awareness, but they rarely focus on reducing emotional pressure in intergenerational communication. Kinlight therefore explores a playful, low-pressure, and asynchronous mobile experience that helps families maintain connection through lightweight sharing, soft emotional awareness, and easier conversation openings. Instead of making communication faster or more frequent, the project focuses on making contact feel easier to start, safer to control, and warmer to return to.

Academic papers

Shin et al. (CSCW 2021), Designing Technologies to Support Parent-Child Relationships
What it does well
  1. Reviews technology-mediated parent-child relationships across multiple contexts.
  2. Highlights emotional complexity and mismatched expectations in parent-child communication.
  3. Shows that relatedness technologies need to consider both relationship maintenance and everyday communication practices.
What it misses
  1. Does not focus specifically on university students and parents living apart.
  2. Does not propose a concrete lightweight mobile interaction for everyday use.
  3. Does not directly address how playful interaction can reduce communication pressure.
Design implication for Kinlight

Kinlight should treat parent-student communication as a relationship-maintenance problem, not just a messaging problem.

Shakeri et al. (CHI 2023), Sensing Their Presence: How Emerging Adults and Their Parents Connect After Moving Apart
What it does well
  1. Explains how emerging adults and parents maintain connection after moving apart.
  2. Highlights subtle awareness and presence as important relationship mechanisms.
  3. Shows that parent-child connection does not always require direct conversation.
What it misses
  1. Does not propose a concrete mobile interaction system for everyday use.
  2. Does not focus specifically on playful communication features.
  3. Does not fully resolve how to balance awareness with privacy and pressure.
Design implication for Kinlight

Kinlight should support soft awareness and low-pressure presence, rather than only direct messaging.

Lee et al. (PACMHCI 2025), DJ-Fam: Using Favorite Songs as a Catalyst for Fostering Communication between Parents and Young Adult Children Living Apart
What it does well
  1. Demonstrates that mediated prompts can help parents and young adult children start conversations.
  2. Shows that shared media can diversify parent-child conversation topics.
  3. Provides a concrete HCI design case for parent-child communication across distance.
What it misses
  1. Focuses on music as the central interaction medium.
  2. Does not cover broader everyday sharing, mood reflection, or virtual companion interaction.
  3. Does not fully address how to keep communication lightweight for repeated everyday use.
Design implication for Kinlight

Kinlight can use prompts as conversation starters, but should support broader everyday cues beyond one media type.

Wenhart et al. (CHI 2025), Relatedness Technologies: An Online Compendium and Systematic Review
What it does well
  1. Reviews a broad set of technologies for supporting emotional connection across distance.
  2. Identifies fragmentation in relatedness technologies.
  3. Emphasizes the need for better-grounded designs and stronger evidence.
What it misses
  1. Does not focus specifically on parent-student communication.
  2. Does not offer a single concrete interaction model for low-pressure intergenerational contact.
  3. Does not resolve how to combine emotional awareness, conversation support, and privacy.
Design implication for Kinlight

Kinlight should integrate relatedness support into a focused everyday system rather than adding isolated emotional features.

Commercial products

WhatsApp
https://www.whatsapp.com/?lang=en
What it does well
  1. Supports private messaging, voice messages, reactions, and media sharing.
  2. Enables asynchronous family communication through a familiar interface.
  3. Allows quick responses without requiring long formal messages.
What it misses
  1. It is a general messaging tool, not designed around parent-student relationship maintenance.
  2. It does not actively reduce the pressure of starting or sustaining family conversation.
  3. It does not provide relationship-specific prompts, emotional reflection, or playful support.
Design implication for Kinlight

Kinlight should not simply become another chat app. It should reduce communication pressure and create warmer entry points for family contact.

Life360
https://www.life360.com/
What it does well
  1. Provides family awareness through location and safety features.
  2. Supports parents’ need for reassurance.
  3. Offers a clear family-oriented use case.
What it misses
  1. Its core logic is safety monitoring rather than emotional connection.
  2. Location/status tracking can feel intrusive for students.
  3. It does not help families start softer conversations or express care more gently.
Design implication for Kinlight

Kinlight should avoid surveillance-style awareness and instead use soft, abstract, consent-based emotional cues.

FamilyAlbum
https://family-album.com/
What it does well
  1. Provides a private family-only space for photo and video sharing.
  2. Makes family memories easy to collect and revisit.
  3. Supports comments and reactions around shared family content.
What it misses
  1. Focuses more on memory archiving than everyday emotional connection.
  2. Does not directly help users start difficult or awkward conversations.
  3. Does not address parent-student tensions around pressure, autonomy, and privacy.
Design implication for Kinlight

Kinlight can borrow private family sharing, but should turn shared moments into low-pressure conversation openings rather than a passive album.

Agape
https://apps.apple.com/us/app/agape-feel-close-when-apart
What it does well
  1. Uses daily questions to spark meaningful conversation.
  2. Helps people feel closer when apart.
  3. Shows that prompts can reduce the difficulty of starting conversation.
What it misses
  1. It is not designed specifically for intergenerational parent-student relationships.
  2. Some prompts may not fit Chinese family communication norms or parent-child dynamics.
  3. Prompting alone does not address emotional privacy, family awareness, or lightweight everyday sharing.
Design implication for Kinlight

Kinlight should use prompts that are contextual, editable, and appropriate for parent-student communication.

Cross-source gap summary

Across the academic and commercial sources, we identified three recurring gaps:

💬 Gap 1

Existing tools support contact, but not low-pressure emotional maintenance.

Messaging and sharing tools help families stay in touch, but they rarely reduce reply pressure or the burden of sustaining parent-student communication.

🌱 Gap 2

Prompt systems help start conversations, but are not tailored to parent-student relationships.

Prompt-based systems can open conversations, but they need to better fit autonomy, tone, and cultural expectations in parent-student communication.

🛡️ Gap 3

Family awareness can reassure, but can also feel like monitoring.

Safety and awareness tools can comfort parents, but may create pressure for students when privacy boundaries are unclear.

Kinlight’s position: a low-pressure intergenerational communication system that combines soft awareness, lightweight expression, and gentle conversation support.

Stakeholders

Who Kinlight Is Designed For

🎓 Primary stakeholders: University students living away from home

Need: Stay connected with parents without forced long replies.

Risk: Family communication becomes another duty or interruption.

Kinlight response: Lightweight sharing, optional reactions, and privacy-aware emotional expression.

👪 Primary stakeholders: Parents living apart from their children

Need: Receive reassurance and maintain warmth without over-checking.

Risk: Care may be perceived as monitoring, pressure, or repeated questioning.

Kinlight response: Soft awareness, gentle prompts, and low-pressure care gestures.

🤝 Relational stakeholder: The parent-student relationship itself

Need: Maintain emotional connection across distance.

Risk: Contact becomes transactional, awkward, or overly problem-focused.

Kinlight response: Shared moments, weekly reflection, and supportive interaction rituals.

🌍 Secondary stakeholders: Wider family members and future family contexts

Need: Support possible expansion to grandparents, siblings, or wider family groups.

Risk: A design that only fits one parent-child pair may not scale to broader family contexts.

Kinlight response: Flexible family spaces and interaction patterns that can extend beyond the current scope.

3 Key Design Requirements

Low Reply Pressure, Flexible Timing, Easy to Start

User Evidence → Pain Point → Design Requirement

Each requirement is grounded in repeated interview evidence rather than added as a decorative feature.

User Evidence

Students preferred quick acknowledgement and often avoided replying when a response felt too effortful.

Pain Point

Everyday contact can feel like a communication task, which increases reply pressure.

Design Requirement

Low Reply Pressure so users can respond lightly without composing a full message.

User Evidence

Parents and students often read updates at different times and wanted more control over when to respond.

Pain Point

Timing mismatch makes synchronous conversation difficult and can create hidden pressure.

Design Requirement

Flexible Timing so sharing and reassurance can happen asynchronously.

User Evidence

Both sides described silence after a few days because they did not know how to start naturally.

Pain Point

Starting contact can feel awkward when there is no clear opening or shared prompt.

Design Requirement

Easy to Start so contact begins from gentle prompts and lightweight shared moments.

Turning Point / Week 8

Week 8 Poster Presentation Feedback

The Week 8 poster presentation became a design checkpoint for Kinlight. The feedback below is paraphrased from the tutors' comments and will guide our next round of reflection and iteration.

What Week 8 Challenged

Feature Differentiation

Is Lightweight Sharing clearly different from existing social media such as WeChat Moments?

AI Authenticity

Should AI write family replies, or should it only guide expression?

Virtual Pet Role

Is the pet only decorative, or does it provide affective support?

Emotional Risk

Could a sad or negative pet state create guilt, anxiety, or pressure?

🧭 Core value and feature differentiation

Tutor Feedback from Dr Ma

  • The first two features may not be enough to change parent-student communication if family members are already reluctant to talk.
  • Lightweight sharing should be clearly differentiated from existing social media features, such as WeChat Moments.
  • The value of AI-assisted replies needs further consideration, because users may find AI-generated family replies inauthentic or emotionally inappropriate.
  • The virtual pet is a promising direction, but its role should be strengthened beyond being cute or decorative.
  • Possible improvements include message delivery, customisation, feeding, or small caring actions.
  • A sad or “down” pet may reinforce a negative family atmosphere rather than help repair it.
🐾 Affective affordance and emotional support

Tutor Feedback from Dr Li

  • The virtual pet is cute and potentially engaging, but its emotional role should be made clearer.
  • The pet should not remain only a decorative feature.
  • It should provide affective affordance by supporting companionship, care, comfort, and emotional connection.
  • Directly visualising a negative family atmosphere through a sad pet may make users feel worse.
  • The pet should offer gentle emotional support rather than simply mirror negative mood.
  • The next iteration should consider how the pet can help users feel reassured, accompanied, and emotionally connected.
↓

Design Consequence

Week 8 feedback became a turning point. We stopped treating Kinlight as three separate features and reframed it as a low-pressure intergenerational communication support system. The next phase uses user feedback and product benchmarking to refine this direction.

Phase 2 / Week 8-10

Reframe Through Evidence

After Week 8, we used demo-based user feedback to test whether the tutor feedback also appeared in real user reactions.

Method Summary

Participants: 3 university students + 3 parents Method: Demo trial + think-aloud + short interview Tasks: Sharing, Gentle Topics, Mood Jar / Pet Focus: Communication value, AI authenticity, privacy, emotional comfort

Feedback-to-Design Implication Matrix

Finding Evidence from users Design implication
Sharing alone is not enough Several users compared Lightweight Sharing to WeChat Moments or a simple “like” system. Make sharing family-specific and conversation-oriented, rather than another passive feed.
AI should guide, not replace expression Both students and parents were cautious about full AI-written family replies and preferred editable suggestions. Use AI for prompt directions, tone guidance, and rewrite support instead of automatic emotional replies.
The pet’s role is promising but unclear Users liked the pet’s warmth but were unsure whether it represented the individual, the family, or the relationship. Define the pet as an affective mediator that supports care, reassurance, and repair.
Negative emotional feedback may create pressure Students associated a sad pet with guilt; parents associated it with anxiety or self-blame. Avoid sad, punitive, or diagnostic pet states. Use supportive and repair-oriented cues.
Privacy boundaries must be explicit Students did not want parents to see detailed mood records; parents needed to know what was private or shared. Keep detailed mood logs private and use only abstract, consent-based family awareness cues.
Parent-side guidance is necessary Parents wanted to care but did not always know how to express concern without pressure. Add softer parent-facing wording, care suggestions, or gentle next-step guidance.
Student Feedback Details

Student Feedback Snapshot

👩‍🎓

Student A

Year 2 daughter, contacts parents 2-3 times per week

What worked
  • Found quick replies less stressful than typing a full message.
  • Saw value in Gentle Topics when she wanted to talk but did not know what to say.
  • Liked the pet as a soft companion if it stayed supportive.
Questions / Concerns
  • Lightweight sharing still felt close to WeChat Moments.
  • Some preset replies felt too emotional or unlike her real tone.
  • AI should give a direction, not write the full message for her.
  • Mood privacy was unclear.
  • A sad pet could create guilt instead of comfort.
Design implication
  • Keep low-effort interaction, but make replies more natural, editable, and privacy-aware.
👨‍🎓

Student B

Year 3 son, contacts parents about once a week

What worked
  • Quick reactions helped him acknowledge family posts without starting a long chat.
  • Context-aware prompts felt more useful than generic emotional prompts.
  • Preferred short, casual follow-up questions based on what parents had shared.
Questions / Concerns
  • Reactions alone may become another “like” system.
  • Generic AI emotional wording felt unnatural.
  • Did not want the system to rate the family relationship.
  • A sad pet could become another emotional responsibility.
  • Wanted only vague availability status, not detailed mood sharing.
Design implication
  • AI should support reply direction and context-aware prompts, not emotional over-expression.
👩‍💻

Student C

Year 1 daughter, frequent contact with mother, less contact with father

What worked
  • Lightweight status sharing could reduce the need to explain everything from the beginning.
  • Gentle Topics seemed useful for starting conversations with her father.
  • Throwing candy into the Mood Jar felt lighter than directly selecting sad or anxious emotions.
Questions / Concerns
  • Did not want read receipts, streaks, or reminders that create duty pressure.
  • Worried parents may not understand abstract labels such as “topic card”.
  • Was confused whether the pet represented herself, the family, or the relationship.
  • A pet representing “bad family atmosphere” felt too heavy.
Design implication
  • Clarify what the pet represents and avoid turning family connection into a measurable duty.

Phase 2 / Week 8-10

Product Benchmarking

After Round 1 feedback, we benchmarked related products and research prototypes to refine Kinlight’s feature differentiation, AI support, pet role, privacy boundaries, and emotional safety.

Why Benchmarking Was Needed

Benchmarking was used to refine—not restart—the design after users questioned feature differentiation, AI authenticity, the pet’s role, and privacy risk.

What Product Benchmarking Revealed

Private Sharing

Shared moments need to become low-pressure conversation openings, not another passive feed.

Prompt Support

AI should support authentic expression through directions and rewrites instead of replacing family replies.

Affective Pet

The pet should support care, reassurance, and repair rather than simply display family mood.

Soft Awareness

Awareness should be abstract, consent-based, and emotionally soft rather than surveillance-style.

Reference Products

Reference Products

FamilyAlbum

Private family photo sharing
What works
  • Supports private family photo and video sharing.
  • Automatically organises family memories by time.
  • Allows comments and family responses around shared photos.
Limitations for Kinlight
  • More suitable for family memory archiving than everyday emotional communication.
  • Does not directly solve awkward conversation opening.
  • Focuses on “sharing memories”, while Kinlight needs low-pressure ongoing connection.
Design takeaway
  • Photos can be a strong family connection medium, but Kinlight should turn daily photos into conversation openings rather than a passive family album.

Locket Widget

Ambient daily sharing
What works
  • Provides lightweight ambient awareness through live photos on the home screen.
  • Makes small daily moments visible without requiring a full conversation.
  • Reduces interaction friction by placing updates directly in a familiar phone space.
Limitations for Kinlight
  • Designed mainly for friends rather than intergenerational family relationships.
  • The interaction can become casual image exchange without deeper emotional support.
  • Does not address AI prompts, emotional privacy, or family-specific tension.
Design takeaway
  • Kinlight can borrow low-friction daily glimpses, but should frame them as family-only emotional cues rather than another image feed.

Agapé

Conversation prompts
What works
  • Uses daily questions to spark meaningful conversations.
  • Helps people feel close when apart.
  • Shows that prompts can reduce the difficulty of starting a conversation.
Limitations for Kinlight
  • Mainly framed around relationship wellness and broad intimate relationships.
  • Some prompts may not fit parent-student communication.
  • Prompting alone may feel artificial if not grounded in users’ real context.
Design takeaway
  • AI or prompt support should act as a conversation starter, not as an automatic reply generator.

Widgetable

Shared virtual pet
What works
  • Uses shared virtual pets to support playful co-care.
  • Allows users to feed, play with, and raise pets together.
  • Includes mood-sharing and distance-based widgets for close relationships.
Limitations for Kinlight
  • Some interactions may feel too playful or couple/friend-oriented for parent-student relationships.
  • Shared pet care could become another task if over-designed.
  • Mood sharing may create pressure if privacy boundaries are unclear.
Design takeaway
  • Kinlight’s pet should have caring interactions, but they must stay lightweight, optional, and emotionally safe.

Kinder World

Gentle wellbeing companion
What works
  • Uses caring for virtual plants as a gentle wellbeing metaphor.
  • Emphasises calm, low-intensity, and judgment-free interaction.
  • Avoids punishment-style feedback and supports emotional comfort.
Limitations for Kinlight
  • Focuses on individual self-care rather than family communication.
  • The plant metaphor does not directly support parent-child interaction.
  • Does not solve conversation initiation.
Design takeaway
  • Kinlight’s pet should avoid negative or guilt-inducing feedback. It should provide supportive and repair-oriented cues.

Life360

Family awareness and safety
What works
  • Provides clear family awareness through location sharing and safety features.
  • Supports parents’ need for reassurance.
  • Has a strong family-oriented use case.
Limitations for Kinlight
  • Its logic is safety monitoring rather than emotional connection.
  • Location and status tracking can easily feel intrusive.
  • This direction conflicts with students’ need for autonomy and privacy.
Design takeaway
  • Kinlight should avoid surveillance-style family awareness. Its awareness should be soft, abstract, and consent-based.

Technical Reflection

Technical Reflection & AI Use

AI was used as a support tool for visual asset generation and front-end implementation. It did not define our user research findings, design requirements, or final design logic. All AI-assisted outputs were reviewed by the team and checked against Kinlight’s intended interaction goals.

AI-generated pixel pet images

P — Prompt

“Generate a cute Maine Coon cat in a lying pose, in pixel-art style.”

The context was that we had already created a white cat asset, so the new pet image needed to match the same visual style, posture, and warm tone used in the Kinlight interface.

O — Outcome

The AI generated a usable pixel-style cat image. The pose and visual mood were close to the existing asset, but the output still needed human checking for consistency, transparency, and whether it fitted the interface without distracting from the portfolio content.

A — Action

We reviewed the image manually, exported suitable versions as PNG assets, and reused similar prompt patterns to generate other pet images. Assets that did not match the UI style were discarded or regenerated. The images were used only as decorative interface assets, not as sources for design requirements.

AI-assisted UI interaction modification

P — Prompt

“Please help me modify the page effect after clicking the ‘Care for Someone’ button. The background should become blurred to highlight the feature card.”

The context was that the Jar page needed a clearer focus state when users opened the shared care view.

O — Outcome

The AI suggested a blur-overlay interaction and related front-end code changes. The proposal matched our intended interaction direction because it visually separated the active card from the background and made the care popup easier to notice.

A — Action

We adapted the suggested code into the front-end implementation, tested the click behaviour, and checked whether the blurred background still preserved readability and clear interaction feedback. After verification, we reused the same interaction pattern for other similar buttons to keep the interface consistent.
