Section 01

Motivation & Research

This section explains the project motivation, research grounding, and target communication context that shaped Kinlight’s design direction.

The Why

Why this track

Kinlight fits the Social Connectivity / Relation between Generations track because it addresses a specific intergenerational communication problem: how parents and university students can stay emotionally connected after moving apart, without turning communication into a burden. The project focuses on a low-pressure, asynchronous, mobile experience that supports lightweight status sharing, quick emotional reactions, and AI-assisted conversation prompts. This direction matches the track well because the goal is not simply to send messages more efficiently, but to design for relationship maintenance, emotional closeness, and sustainable everyday interaction between generations. This positioning is also consistent with recent HCI work showing that parent-child communication after moving apart is shaped by subtle awareness, uneven expectations, and the need for better relatedness technologies.

The Gap

Research gap

Across both research and products, the main gap is clear: existing tools tend to support messaging, safety awareness, or memory sharing separately, leaving limited support for combining low-pressure emotional awareness, lightweight reactions, and easier conversation opening in one parent-student communication experience. Kinlight is designed to address this gap.

Stakeholder Scope

Stakeholders

The project focuses on university students living away from home, parents living apart from their children, the parent-student relationship itself, and wider family members or future family contexts. The detailed student and parent personas are presented in the User Research & Requirements section.

Research Gap

Academic Papers and Commercial Products

This block separates research grounding from product comparison while keeping the summary concise and scannable.

Academic papers

Shin et al. (CSCW 2021), Designing Technologies to Support Parent-Child Relationships

This paper reviews technology-mediated parent-child relationships and highlights recurring issues such as mismatched expectations and emotional complexity. It provides a strong overview, but it does not turn these insights into a lightweight design specifically for parents and university students living apart.

Shakeri et al. (CHI 2023), Sensing Their Presence: How Emerging Adults and Their Parents Connect After Moving Apart

This paper studies how emerging adults and parents maintain connection through subtle forms of presence after separation. It is highly relevant to our topic, but it is more diagnostic than solution-oriented and stops short of proposing a simple everyday mobile interaction model.

Lee et al. (PACMHCI 2025), DJ-Fam: Using Favorite Songs as a Catalyst for Fostering Communication between Parents and Young Adult Children Living Apart

This paper presents a mobile system that uses shared music as a conversation starter for parents and young adult children. It shows the value of mediated prompts, but its interaction is built around music specifically, leaving room for a broader communication design that supports everyday mood sharing and low-effort check-ins beyond one activity domain.

Wenhart et al. (CHI 2025), Relatedness Technologies: An Online Compendium and Systematic Review

This paper reviews a large body of technologies designed to support emotional connection across distance. It concludes that the field remains fragmented and often lacks strong grounding and robust evidence, which suggests a need for simpler, more focused, and more practically usable designs like ours.

Commercial products

WhatsApp
https://www.whatsapp.com/?lang=en

WhatsApp supports private messaging, Status, reactions, and voice messages. It is similar to our project in enabling asynchronous family contact, but it is a general messenger and does not actively reduce the pressure of starting or sustaining parent-child conversations.

Life360
https://www.life360.com/

Life360 supports family location sharing and safety-oriented awareness. It overlaps with our project in offering passive family awareness, but its core logic is monitoring and safety rather than emotional expression or low-pressure relationship building.

FamilyAlbum
https://family-album.com/

FamilyAlbum offers private photo and video sharing with comments and reactions. It is relevant because it supports lightweight family sharing, but it is centered on memory archiving rather than helping parents and university students maintain everyday emotional connection in the present.

Agape
https://apps.apple.com/us/app/agape-feel-close-when-apart

Agape uses daily questions to spark meaningful conversation and help people feel close when apart. This is close to our idea of prompt-supported communication, but the product is not designed specifically for intergenerational relationships and does not address the distinct communication dynamics between parents and university students.

Gap summary

Across both research and products, the main gap is clear: existing tools tend to support messaging, safety awareness, or memory sharing separately, leaving limited support for combining low-pressure emotional awareness, lightweight reactions, and easier conversation opening in one parent-student communication experience. Kinlight is designed to address this gap.

Stakeholders

Who Kinlight Is Designed For

The stakeholder framing focuses on relationship maintenance rather than only individual messaging behaviour.

Primary stakeholders: University students living away from home

These users are students who want to stay connected with their parents, but often avoid frequent or effortful communication because of busy schedules, emotional reserve, or uncertainty about what to say. For them, the value of the project is a lighter way to maintain warmth and contact without creating high reply pressure.

Primary stakeholders: Parents living apart from their children

Parents are central users because they often want more regular connection but may worry about interrupting, over-checking, or receiving very limited updates. The project offers them softer forms of awareness and easier ways to open communication without making interaction feel controlling or demanding.

Relational stakeholder: The parent-student relationship itself

The project is not only for two individual users; it is for the relationship between them. This relationship is affected by distance, mismatched expectations, and reduced everyday co-presence. Kinlight aims to support the relationship by improving the quality, ease, and emotional sustainability of staying in touch.

Secondary stakeholders: Wider family members and future family contexts

Although the current scope focuses on parents and university students, the design may later extend to other family relationships, such as grandparents and grandchildren or siblings living apart. Considering these groups helps keep the system flexible and inclusive rather than overly narrow.

3 Playful Must-Haves

Based on interview findings and the journey map, we translated recurring communication pain points into three playful requirements.

Core interaction qualities that keep Kinlight warm, low-pressure, and easy to return to across everyday family routines.

☁️

Low Reply Pressure

The system must support lightweight emotional check-ins without creating an obligation for immediate or lengthy replies. Interactions should feel gentle and caring rather than demanding, for example through no read receipts, mild reactions, and low-effort sharing.

🕒

Flexible Timing

The system must work asynchronously and fit naturally into the daily routines of both parents and university students. Users should be able to share, view, and respond at their own pace, reducing scheduling conflict, reply pressure, and guilt caused by delayed responses.

✨

Easy to Start

The system must make conversations easier to initiate, especially when users want to connect but do not know what to say. Playful entry points such as AI-guided topic cards, mood sharing, emojis, and photo-based interaction should lower the barrier to starting contact.

User Research Evidence

We conducted remote semi-structured interviews with target users. The screenshots document the interview sessions and our note-taking process. Any AI-assisted meeting summaries were used only as supporting notes and were checked against our own discussion records.

Section 03

Ideation & Alternatives

This section should show how ideas were generated, compared, and translated into an early prototype direction.

Crazy Eights

Eight fast concept sketches were explored to test different ways of supporting low-pressure family connection. The cards below document both promising directions and ideas that were intentionally set aside.

8 early concepts

🫙 Emotion Jar

Exploratory concept

Users can privately drop a mood ball each day to reflect their feelings over time, while the jar provides a gentle visual trace of emotional patterns.

📸 Home-Based Lightweight Sharing

Promising direction

Family members can post small daily moments, such as photos or short updates, while others respond through lightweight reactions or brief replies.

🎨 Shared Emotion Calendar

Rejected

A multi-person emotion calendar allows family members to log their daily moods using color, making long-term individual and collective emotional patterns visible over time.

Why it was rejected: This concept made emotions too explicit. It risked creating a sense of monitoring, increasing pressure to explain one’s feelings, and discouraging honest self-reporting.

🌳 Family Tree Interaction Hub

Rejected

A family tree interface acts as a shared home page where each member node supports messages, care reminders, or interaction prompts to encourage family contact.

Why it was rejected: This concept risked turning communication into a task-like activity, increasing pressure and making the experience feel less lightweight and natural.

💬 Direct Family Chat with AI Quick Replies

Rejected

A standard family chat interface supported by AI-generated quick replies for faster everyday communication.

Why it was rejected: This concept remained too close to existing chat applications and did not sufficiently reduce reply pressure.

📔 Collaborative Family Journal

Rejected

A shared digital journal where family members co-create entries, memories, and reflections together over time.

Why it was rejected: This idea required too much effort and time, making it unsuitable for lightweight everyday communication.

🤖 AI Family Persona Chat

Rejected

Users can chat with an AI-generated version of a family member that imitates their tone and helps initiate conversation.

Why it was rejected: This concept felt less authentic and raised clear privacy and trust concerns.

🐣 Shared Companion Mood Indicator

Exploratory concept

A playful shared companion reflects the family’s recent emotional activity and interaction level, providing an ambient and indirect awareness cue.

Selection Criteria

We selected ideas for the current prototype based on five criteria: low reply pressure, flexible timing, ease of conversation initiation, emotional privacy, and feasibility for front-end implementation.

Design Decision

  • Lightweight Sharing — Selected: Supports low-effort daily updates and quick emotional response.
  • Gentle Topics — Selected: Helps users start conversations without needing to think of a message from scratch.
  • Private Mood Jar — Selected: Supports emotional self-reflection while preserving privacy.
  • Shared Emotion Calendar — Not selected: Made emotions too explicit and could create monitoring pressure.
  • AI Family Persona Chat — Not selected: Raised authenticity, privacy, and trust concerns.
  • Collaborative Family Journal — Not selected: Required too much effort for lightweight everyday communication.

Section 05

Week 8 Tutor Feedback

The Week 8 poster presentation became a design checkpoint for Kinlight. The feedback below is paraphrased from the tutors' comments and will guide our next round of reflection and iteration.

🧭 Core value and feature differentiation

Tutor Feedback from Dr Ma

  • The first two features may not be enough to change parent-student communication if family members are already reluctant to talk.
  • Lightweight sharing should be clearly differentiated from existing social media features, such as WeChat Moments.
  • The value of AI-assisted replies needs further consideration, because users may find AI-generated family replies inauthentic or emotionally inappropriate.
  • The virtual pet is a promising direction, but its role should be strengthened beyond being cute or decorative.
  • Possible improvements include message delivery, customisation, feeding, or small caring actions.
  • A sad or “down” pet may reinforce a negative family atmosphere rather than help repair it.
🐾 Affective affordance and emotional support

Tutor Feedback from Dr Li

  • The virtual pet is cute and potentially engaging, but its emotional role should be made clearer.
  • The pet should not remain only a decorative feature.
  • It should provide affective affordance by supporting companionship, care, comfort, and emotional connection.
  • Directly visualising a negative family atmosphere through a sad pet may make users feel worse.
  • The pet should offer gentle emotional support rather than simply mirror negative mood.
  • The next iteration should consider how the pet can help users feel reassured, accompanied, and emotionally connected.

Section 06

Round 1: Demo-Based User Feedback

After the Week 8 tutor feedback, we conducted a short demo-based feedback session with student and parent users to understand whether the identified risks also appeared in real user reactions.

Method Summary

Participants: 3 university students + 3 parents Method: Demo trial + think-aloud + short interview Tasks: Sharing, Gentle Topics, Mood Jar / Pet Focus: Communication value, AI authenticity, privacy, emotional comfort

Student Feedback Snapshot

👩‍🎓

Student A

Year 2 daughter, contacts parents 2-3 times per week

What worked
  • Found quick replies less stressful than typing a full message.
  • Saw value in Gentle Topics when she wanted to talk but did not know what to say.
  • Liked the pet as a soft companion if it stayed supportive.
Questions / Concerns
  • Lightweight sharing still felt close to WeChat Moments.
  • Some preset replies felt too emotional or unlike her real tone.
  • AI should give a direction, not write the full message for her.
  • Mood privacy was unclear.
  • A sad pet could create guilt instead of comfort.
Design implication
  • Keep low-effort interaction, but make replies more natural, editable, and privacy-aware.
👨‍🎓

Student B

Year 3 son, contacts parents about once a week

What worked
  • Quick reactions helped him acknowledge family posts without starting a long chat.
  • Context-aware prompts felt more useful than generic emotional prompts.
  • Preferred short, casual follow-up questions based on what parents had shared.
Questions / Concerns
  • Reactions alone may become another “like” system.
  • Generic AI emotional wording felt unnatural.
  • Did not want the system to rate the family relationship.
  • A sad pet could become another emotional responsibility.
  • Wanted only vague availability status, not detailed mood sharing.
Design implication
  • AI should support reply direction and context-aware prompts, not emotional over-expression.
👩‍💻

Student C

Year 1 daughter, frequent contact with mother, less contact with father

What worked
  • Lightweight status sharing could reduce the need to explain everything from the beginning.
  • Gentle Topics seemed useful for starting conversations with her father.
  • Throwing candy into the Mood Jar felt lighter than directly selecting sad or anxious emotions.
Questions / Concerns
  • Did not want read receipts, streaks, or reminders that create duty pressure.
  • Worried parents may not understand abstract labels such as “topic card”.
  • Was confused whether the pet represented herself, the family, or the relationship.
  • A pet representing “bad family atmosphere” felt too heavy.
Design implication
  • Clarify what the pet represents and avoid turning family connection into a measurable duty.

Parent Feedback Snapshot

👩‍🦱

Parent A

Mother, 45-50, wants to know child’s state without sounding intrusive

What worked
  • Felt reassured by small daily updates from her child.
  • Found AI rewrite suggestions useful for making care sound softer.
  • Liked the idea of sending one gentle message instead of many questions.
Questions / Concerns
  • A simple reaction was not enough to express parental care.
  • AI wording could sound too polished or unlike her real voice.
  • A sad pet would make her anxious and likely to ask more questions.
  • Preferred clear guidance such as “give her some space today”.
Design implication
  • Parent-side support should help reframe care into gentle language and provide clear next steps.
👨‍🦳

Parent B

Father, 50-55, has fewer conversations with son and often only asks about study

What worked
  • Topic suggestions outside study were useful.
  • Prompts could remind him not to ask only about academic performance.
  • Editable suggestions felt acceptable if they preserved his own tone.
Questions / Concerns
  • Initially understood the app as a “family version of Moments”.
  • Worried AI might read or analyse private family conversations.
  • Did not understand why throwing candy represented mood.
  • Pet feedback was cute but not actionable.
  • Needed to know what he should do after seeing a low pet state.
Design implication
  • Reduce explicit “AI” framing, explain data use clearly, and turn pet feedback into actionable guidance.
👩‍🦰

Parent C

Mother, 40-45, frequent contact with daughter but sometimes causes small conflicts

What worked
  • Liked prompts that encouraged parents to share their own day before asking questions.
  • Saw value in helping parents change how they express concern.
  • Felt the pet could become a warm family mediator.
Questions / Concerns
  • Worried the app could feel like another monitoring tool for the child.
  • Some gentle replies felt too Western or unlike Chinese family communication.
  • A sad pet could make parents feel guilty or judged.
  • Wanted repair-oriented suggestions rather than negative mood display.
Design implication
  • The pet should support repair and reassurance, not diagnose or visualise family problems.

What Round 1 Revealed

↔

Sharing alone is not enough

  • Several users compared Lightweight Sharing to WeChat Moments or a simple “like” system.
  • The design needs to emphasise low-pressure family communication, not just content sharing.
✎

AI should guide, not replace expression

  • Both students and parents were cautious about AI-written family replies.
  • Users preferred editable prompts, rewrite suggestions, or conversation directions.
🐾

The pet’s role is promising but unclear

  • Users liked the pet’s warmth, but were unsure whether it represented the individual, the family, or the relationship.
  • The pet should become an affective mediator rather than a family mood score.
↓

Negative emotional feedback may create pressure

  • Students associated a sad pet with guilt.
  • Parents associated it with anxiety or self-blame.
  • The pet should provide supportive or repair-oriented cues instead of directly showing negative family atmosphere.
◌

Privacy boundaries must be explicit

  • Students did not want parents to see detailed mood records.
  • Parents needed to understand what information was private, abstract, or shared.
☼

Parent-side guidance is necessary

  • Parents often wanted to care but did not know how to express concern without pressure.
  • The system should help parents ask less intrusively and share more of their own everyday life.

Next Step: Product Benchmarking

Round 1 showed that Kinlight should not be framed as only a family sharing app. Its stronger direction is a low-pressure intergenerational communication support system that helps students and parents maintain everyday connection, initiate gentler conversations, and receive emotional companionship without turning family care into monitoring or pressure.

Section 07

Product Benchmarking

After Round 1, we reviewed existing products and research prototypes to understand how similar systems handle private sharing, conversation prompts, AI support, emotional companions, and family monitoring. This helped us identify what Kinlight should borrow, avoid, or redesign for parent-student communication.

Why Benchmarking Was Needed

Round 1 showed that Kinlight should not become only another family sharing feed. Users questioned whether lightweight sharing was different from WeChat Moments, whether AI-generated replies felt authentic, whether the virtual pet had a clear role, and whether negative pet feedback might create pressure. We therefore reviewed reference products and research work to identify design patterns that could inform the next iteration.

Reference Products

FamilyAlbum

Private family photo sharing
What works
  • Supports private family photo and video sharing.
  • Automatically organises family memories by time.
  • Allows comments and family responses around shared photos.
Limitations for Kinlight
  • More suitable for family memory archiving than everyday emotional communication.
  • Does not directly solve awkward conversation opening.
  • Focuses on “sharing memories”, while Kinlight needs low-pressure ongoing connection.
Design takeaway
  • Photos can be a strong family connection medium, but Kinlight should turn daily photos into conversation openings rather than a passive family album.

Locket Widget

Ambient daily sharing
What works
  • Provides lightweight ambient awareness through live photos on the home screen.
  • Makes small daily moments visible without requiring a full conversation.
  • Reduces interaction friction by placing updates directly in a familiar phone space.
Limitations for Kinlight
  • Designed mainly for friends rather than intergenerational family relationships.
  • The interaction can become casual image exchange without deeper emotional support.
  • Does not address AI prompts, emotional privacy, or family-specific tension.
Design takeaway
  • Kinlight can borrow low-friction daily glimpses, but should frame them as family-only emotional cues rather than another image feed.

Agapé

Conversation prompts
What works
  • Uses daily questions to spark meaningful conversations.
  • Helps people feel close when apart.
  • Shows that prompts can reduce the difficulty of starting a conversation.
Limitations for Kinlight
  • Mainly framed around relationship wellness and broad intimate relationships.
  • Some prompts may not fit parent-student communication.
  • Prompting alone may feel artificial if not grounded in users’ real context.
Design takeaway
  • AI or prompt support should act as a conversation starter, not as an automatic reply generator.

Widgetable

Shared virtual pet
What works
  • Uses shared virtual pets to support playful co-care.
  • Allows users to feed, play with, and raise pets together.
  • Includes mood-sharing and distance-based widgets for close relationships.
Limitations for Kinlight
  • Some interactions may feel too playful or couple/friend-oriented for parent-student relationships.
  • Shared pet care could become another task if over-designed.
  • Mood sharing may create pressure if privacy boundaries are unclear.
Design takeaway
  • Kinlight’s pet should have caring interactions, but they must stay lightweight, optional, and emotionally safe.

Kinder World

Gentle wellbeing companion
What works
  • Uses caring for virtual plants as a gentle wellbeing metaphor.
  • Emphasises calm, low-intensity, and judgment-free interaction.
  • Avoids punishment-style feedback and supports emotional comfort.
Limitations for Kinlight
  • Focuses on individual self-care rather than family communication.
  • The plant metaphor does not directly support parent-child interaction.
  • Does not solve conversation initiation.
Design takeaway
  • Kinlight’s pet should avoid negative or guilt-inducing feedback. It should provide supportive and repair-oriented cues.

Life360

Family awareness and safety
What works
  • Provides clear family awareness through location sharing and safety features.
  • Supports parents’ need for reassurance.
  • Has a strong family-oriented use case.
Limitations for Kinlight
  • Its logic is safety monitoring rather than emotional connection.
  • Location and status tracking can easily feel intrusive.
  • This direction conflicts with students’ need for autonomy and privacy.
Design takeaway
  • Kinlight should avoid surveillance-style family awareness. Its awareness should be soft, abstract, and consent-based.

Research References

DJ-Fam: Using Favorite Songs as a Catalyst for Fostering Communication between Parents and Young Adult Children Living Apart

HCI research / parent-child communication
What it shows
  • Parent-child communication can be supported by turning everyday media into conversation starters.
  • The value is not the media itself, but how it creates a natural reason to talk.
Design takeaway
  • Kinlight can treat photos, emotions, and pets as gentle communication triggers rather than standalone features.

Design practice: exploring how a virtual pet mobile application helps older adults establish social relationships

Virtual pet / social connection
What it shows
  • Virtual pets can support social connection when designed around user needs and low technical burden.
  • Daily virtual-pet interactions can help users develop responsibility, attachment, and expectations for growth or change.
Design takeaway
  • Kinlight’s pet interaction should be simple, emotionally meaningful, and easy for parents to understand.

What Product Benchmarking Revealed

Private sharing needs a stronger communication role

  • FamilyAlbum and Locket show that photos can create lightweight awareness.
  • Kinlight should avoid becoming another feed and instead turn shared moments into low-pressure conversation openings.

Prompts should support authenticity

  • Agapé and DJ-Fam show that prompts can help people start meaningful conversations.
  • Kinlight should use AI to suggest directions or rewrite tone, not to replace genuine family expression.

The pet should become an affective mediator

  • Widgetable shows that shared pet care can create playful emotional interaction.
  • Kinder World shows that caring metaphors should feel calm and non-judgmental.
  • Kinlight’s pet should support care, reassurance, and repair rather than simply display family mood.

Awareness must not become monitoring

  • Life360 shows the value of family awareness, but also highlights the risk of surveillance-style design.
  • Kinlight should use abstract, consent-based, and emotionally soft awareness.

These references helped us reframe Kinlight from a family sharing app into a low-pressure intergenerational communication system. The next step is to translate these lessons into five revised feature directions for Round 2 concept selection.
